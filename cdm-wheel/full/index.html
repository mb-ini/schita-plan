Product classification across five standards

169 leaves: every OTC product ANNA-DSB registers, and every ISO 10962 group outside the categories those products occupy.

About this page

A catalogue of financial products, seen through five standards

Banks trade hundreds of kinds of financial products, and every system that touches one describes it in its own way. Five international standards try to bring order, and they do not agree with each other. This page lines up 169 products: the 110 over-the-counter derivative templates that the official registry publishes, plus 59 groups of listed and cash instruments such as shares, bonds, funds and exchange-traded futures and options. For each one it shows what the five standards say, where they agree, where they do not, and where they say nothing.

Why it matters

The same product, described in five languages

An interest-rate swap passes through a bank’s trading system, its risk engine, its regulatory reporting and the market-data feeds it buys. Each of them names and structures the product differently, and every connection between two systems ends up with its own hand-made translation, which can be wrong in its own way. A canonical model is a single internal vocabulary with explicit, checkable translations to each outside standard. Before building one, you need to see how far apart the standards really are.

UPI · ANNA-DSB

ISO 4914

The official identity of an over-the-counter derivative, used in regulatory reporting. A strict tree: asset class, instrument type, product. It does not cover listed instruments.

CFI

ISO 10962

A six-character classification code for any instrument. Each position answers its own question, so it is a set of facets, not a tree.

CDM

ISDA / FINOS

A common model of trades. It does not list product types; it decides them with rules that read the trade’s terms.

FpML

XML for derivatives

The format banks use to confirm derivative trades with each other. A product is a composition of building blocks.

FIBO

EDM Council

An ontology of financial terms. A class can have several parents, so it does not fit into any single tree.

Because the five have different shapes (a tree, a code, a rule set, a composition, an ontology), no single hierarchy is true for all of them at once. So the page does not impose one. It fixes the list of products and lets each standard say what it says about each of them.

How much each standard says

Answers, gaps, and questions that do not apply

For every product, each standard either answers, does not apply (the UPI registry, for example, does not cover listed shares), or could have answered and does not. Counting the last two apart matters: otherwise three standards would look as if they fail on a third of the catalogue where nobody asked them.

How the links were made

Derived from the standards, and every claim points to a file and a line

Each standard was downloaded into the project and kept unchanged; every file is checked against its recorded hash. The links on this page are not typed in by hand. They are derived from those files: the UPI side from the registry’s own templates; the CFI code from the registry’s instrument type and by matching attribute names against the CFI standard’s own names; CDM, FpML and FIBO by name, with the kind of match recorded on every row. The few judgement calls are written down, each with its reason.

Every link cites the exact file and line it rests on, and a checking program opens every citation each time the page is built. Not every link is equally strong, so each one is labelled. These are the labels the Lanes view uses:

    A model of our own

    Classification is a field, structure is the type

    On top of the comparison sits a first canonical model of the project’s own: 29 classes, 59 attributes and 5 enumerations, named after CDM where CDM has the concept and after FpML or FIRDS where it does not. Each one maps back to the five standards, with the strength of the link and the line it was read from.

    Two ideas are kept apart on purpose. Classification (UPI template, CFI code, asset class) is a field of a product, so a product can be reclassified without being rebuilt. Structure is what the product is made of: a set of payment legs and the terms they share. An interest-rate swap and a cross-currency swap have the same structure and differ by one rule in CDM: do the two legs pay in different currencies?

    Limits

    What not to rely on

    • This is a research prototype, not a product. Nobody outside the automated process has yet reviewed the results against known trades.
    • Products are at the registry’s coarse granularity: a swaption is one template, whether it is European, Bermudan or American.
    • Links matched by name can be wrong in both directions; the label on each link says how much it rests on.
    • The own model is small next to the catalogue, and the building blocks stop at a fixed depth, so it cannot yet validate a real FpML document.
    • The CFI descriptions come from a public secondary source (iotafinance.com), cross-checked against Wikipedia where possible. ISO 10962 itself is a paid standard and is not included. The FpML schemas are version 5.14, a working draft.
    Glossary

    The terms, briefly

    OTC
    Over the counter: a contract agreed directly between two parties, not on an exchange.
    Listed
    Traded on an exchange: shares, bonds, exchange-traded futures and options.
    IRS
    Interest-rate swap: one party pays a fixed rate, the other a floating rate, in the same currency.
    CIRS
    Cross-currency interest-rate swap: the same, with the two legs in different currencies.
    Swaption
    An option to enter into a swap.
    UPI / ANNA-DSB
    The Unique Product Identifier for OTC derivatives (ISO 4914), issued by the ANNA Derivatives Service Bureau from product templates.
    CFI
    Classification of Financial Instruments (ISO 10962): a six-character code.
    FpML
    Financial products Markup Language: the XML format for derivative trade confirmations.
    CDM
    Common Domain Model: ISDA’s shared model of trades and their life-cycle events.
    FIBO
    Financial Industry Business Ontology from the EDM Council: formal definitions of financial terms.
    FIRDS
    ESMA’s register of reference data for listed instruments in the EU, published in the ISO 20022 format.
    Canonical model
    An organisation’s single internal vocabulary, with explicit translations to each outside format.
